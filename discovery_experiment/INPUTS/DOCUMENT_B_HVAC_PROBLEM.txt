Adaptive Fresh-Air Ventilation Control for Office Buildings

Problem Statement

A mid-size office building (200 occupants, 5 floors, mixed climate with hot humid summers and cold dry winters) uses a variable-air-volume (VAV) HVAC system with outdoor air dampers. The current ventilation control strategy uses a fixed schedule: dampers are set to a minimum position during occupied hours and closed during unoccupied hours. A CO2 sensor in the return air duct provides feedback, but the control logic is simple: if CO2 exceeds 1000 ppm, increase damper position; if it drops below 800 ppm, decrease damper position.

Current Limitations

1. Slow response: The CO2 sensor in the return air duct has significant transport delay (2-5 minutes) because air must travel from occupied zones through the return duct to the sensor. By the time high CO2 is detected, occupants may have already experienced degraded air quality.

2. Overshoot: The simple threshold-based control causes the damper to open too far when CO2 is high, over-ventilating the space and wasting energy. It then closes too far when CO2 drops, causing CO2 to rise again. This produces oscillatory behavior.

3. Inability to anticipate: The system cannot predict when CO2 will rise (e.g., when a meeting starts or when occupancy increases suddenly). It only reacts after the fact.

4. Energy waste: Over-ventilation during periods of low occupancy wastes heating and cooling energy. The building's energy bills are 30% higher than comparable buildings with demand-controlled ventilation.

5. Comfort complaints: During transition seasons (spring/fall), the system struggles to maintain both temperature and air quality, leading to occupant complaints about stuffiness.

Constraints

- Damper actuation rate: The outdoor air damper can change position at most 10% per minute (physical actuator limit).
- Sensor latency: The CO2 sensor has a 2-5 minute transport delay.
- Comfort standards: ASHRAE 62.1 requires minimum ventilation rates per occupant. CO2 should not exceed 1000 ppm during occupied hours.
- Energy target: The building manager wants to reduce ventilation-related energy consumption by at least 15% compared to the fixed-schedule baseline.
- No new sensors: The building has one CO2 sensor (return duct), one temperature sensor (return duct), and outdoor air temperature/humidity sensors. No additional sensors can be installed due to budget constraints.
- Control hardware: The building automation system (BAS) can execute control logic at 1-minute intervals.

What is needed

A ventilation control strategy that:
1. Responds faster than the CO2 sensor transport delay allows
2. Avoids the overshoot/oscillation problem of threshold-based control
3. Can anticipate changes in ventilation demand
4. Minimizes energy consumption while maintaining air quality
5. Works with the existing sensor suite and actuator constraints
6. Does not require a building occupancy model or schedule (occupancy is unpredictable in this building)

The strategy should be implementable on the existing BAS within one month. It should not require cloud connectivity or machine learning infrastructure. It should be tunable by a building engineer without specialized control theory expertise.
