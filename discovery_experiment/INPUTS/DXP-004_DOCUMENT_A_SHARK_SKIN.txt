Shark Skin Denticle Morphology and Hydrodynamic Function

Elasmobranch fishes (sharks and rays) possess a unique integumentary structure fundamentally different from the overlapping scales of teleost fishes. The shark's skin is covered with placoid scales, also called dermal denticles, which are tooth-like structures embedded in the dermis. Each denticle consists of a crown with longitudinal ridges (riblets) separated by valleys, supported by a neck and a base anchored in the dermis.

Denticle Crown Morphology

The crown of each denticle typically features 3 to 7 longitudinal riblets running in the streamwise direction (from nose to tail of the shark). The riblets are raised ridges with a characteristic spacing of 30-100 micrometers between ridge peaks, depending on the species and body location. The riblet height is typically 20-50 micrometers. The cross-sectional shape varies: some species have U-shaped valleys, others have V-shaped or scalloped valleys. The crown surface is enamel-covered, creating a hard, smooth surface at the microscale.

The denticles are arranged in a staggered pattern across the shark's body, with the crowns overlapping like roof tiles. This arrangement creates a continuous ribbed surface at the macroscale, with the riblets aligned in the flow direction during steady swimming.

Hydrodynamic Function

The riblet structure serves multiple hydrodynamic functions:

1. Drag reduction: In turbulent flow, the riblets interact with the near-wall turbulent structures. The streamwise vortices that form in the viscous sublayer of a turbulent boundary layer are the primary source of turbulent drag. The riblets lift these vortices away from the surface, reducing the wetted surface area that the vortices can act upon. The vortices sit on the riblet peaks rather than in the valleys, reducing the shear stress transfer to the surface.

2. Flow directionality: The anisotropic riblet structure creates higher resistance to cross-flow (perpendicular to the riblets) than to streamwise flow. This helps maintain a clean boundary layer and delays flow separation, which is critical for maneuverability.

3. Anti-fouling: The microscale roughness pattern inhibits the attachment of barnacles, algae, and other fouling organisms. The narrow valleys between riblets are too small for most fouling organisms to gain a foothold.

The Mucus Layer

Shark skin also produces a thin layer of mucus (glycoprotein secretion) that covers the denticle surface. The mucus has lubricating properties and may contribute to drag reduction by creating a compliant, low-friction interface between the skin and the water. However, the mucus contribution to overall drag reduction is considered secondary to the riblet geometry. The mucus is continuously secreted and washed away, making it difficult to quantify its effect independently.

Skin Flexibility

The shark's skin is not rigid — it is a flexible, composite structure with collagen fibers arranged in a cross-helical pattern. This flexibility allows the skin to deform under hydrodynamic loading, potentially absorbing pressure fluctuations and reducing the transfer of turbulent stresses to the body. Some researchers have suggested that skin compliance contributes to drag reduction, but this effect is difficult to separate from the riblet effect in living sharks.

Known Failure Modes

1. Low Reynolds number: At low swimming speeds (Re < 10,000), the boundary layer is laminar and the riblets have no effect on drag. The riblets only function in turbulent flow where streamwise vortices are present.

2. Biofouling: In aquaculture or captivity, sharks can accumulate fouling organisms that fill the riblet valleys, negating the hydrodynamic benefit.

3. Riblet damage: Physical damage to the denticle crowns (from abrasion or parasite attachment) can destroy the riblet geometry and locally increase drag.

4. Off-design flow: When the flow direction is not aligned with the riblets (e.g., during sharp turns), the riblets can increase drag rather than reduce it. The anisotropic structure that helps in steady swimming becomes a liability in unsteady maneuvering.

5. Size scaling: The riblet dimensions (30-100 μm spacing) are optimized for the shark's swimming speed and body size. At different scales (much larger or smaller), the same riblet proportions may not produce the same drag reduction effect, because the dimensionless flow parameters change.

What Is Not Known

- The independent contribution of riblets vs. mucus vs. flexibility to total drag reduction has not been fully decomposed in live sharks.
- The optimal riblet cross-sectional shape (U, V, scalloped) for maximum drag reduction is still debated.
- The interaction between riblet geometry and different turbulence intensities (Reynolds number effects) is not fully characterized for biological riblets.
- The long-term durability of the drag reduction effect as denticles wear and regenerate is not quantified.
